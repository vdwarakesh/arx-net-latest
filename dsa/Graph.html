Graph

A Graph is a non-linear data structure used to represent relationships and connections between objects. It consists of vertices (also called nodes) and edges (also called links) that connect pairs of vertices.

Think of a social network. Each person is a vertex, and a friendship or follow relationship between two people is an edge. Unlike a hierarchy, a graph can represent connections in many directions, allowing one person to connect to many others, and those connections can form complicated networks.

The real power of a graph is that it models relationships rather than simply storing individual items. Cities connected by roads, computers connected through a network, and web pages connected by hyperlinks can all be represented as graphs.


Important Terminology

Vertex: An individual entity in the network, such as a city, person, or computer.

Edge: A connection between two vertices, such as a road between cities or a link between web pages.

Adjacent Vertices: Two vertices that share an edge.

Degree: The number of edges connected to a vertex in an undirected graph. In directed graphs, we distinguish between incoming edges (in-degree) and outgoing edges (out-degree).

Path: A sequence of vertices connected by edges, representing a possible route through the network.

Cycle: A path that returns to its starting vertex without repeating other vertices along the way.

Connected Graph: An undirected graph in which every vertex can be reached from every other vertex.


Directed and Undirected Graphs

In a directed graph, edges have a direction. For example, if one social media account follows another, that does not necessarily mean the second account follows the first.

In an undirected graph, edges have no direction. For example, a mutual friendship can be represented as a connection that works both ways.


Weighted and Unweighted Graphs

A weighted graph assigns a value to each edge, such as distance, travel time, cost, or network capacity. An unweighted graph represents connections without assigning numerical weights to them.


Other Important Graph Properties

Cyclic Graph: A graph that contains at least one cycle.

Acyclic Graph: A graph that contains no cycles.

DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph): A directed graph with no directed cycles. It is useful for representing dependencies where something must happen before something else, such as tasks in a project or prerequisites in a course.

Sparse Graph: A graph with relatively few edges compared with the number of possible edges.

Dense Graph: A graph with many edges relative to the number of possible edges.

Bipartite Graph: A graph whose vertices can be divided into two groups so that every edge connects vertices from different groups. For example, a job-matching system connecting applicants to available jobs.

Complete Graph: A simple graph in which every pair of distinct vertices is connected by an edge.

Tree: A connected, undirected graph with no cycles. Trees are a special kind of graph with a particularly restricted structure.


How Are Graphs Represented in Computers?

Adjacency List: An adjacency list stores the neighbors of each vertex. Imagine keeping a contact list for every person that records whom they are connected to.

Adjacency Matrix: An adjacency matrix uses a two-dimensional table to record whether pairs of vertices are connected, and possibly the weight of each connection. Imagine a spreadsheet where rows and columns represent cities, and each cell indicates whether a road connects them.


Where Are Graphs Used in the Real World?

Navigation: Maps represent locations as vertices and roads as edges, with distances or travel times represented by weights.

Social Networks: People or accounts are vertices, while friendships, follows, and interactions are edges.

Computer Networks: Devices are vertices, and communication links are edges.

Project Planning: Tasks are vertices, and dependency relationships form directed edges. A DAG can represent tasks that must be completed in a particular order.

Recommendation Systems: Users and products can be represented as connected vertices, helping systems identify relevant recommendations.

Web Search: Web pages are vertices, and hyperlinks are directed edges that connect one page to another.


Summary

A Graph is a flexible structure for representing objects and the relationships between them. Whether you are mapping roads, modeling friendships, planning projects, or connecting computers, graphs help you understand how a network is organized and how its elements relate to one another.
