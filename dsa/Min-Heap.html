Min-Heap

A Min-Heap is a special type of binary heap in which every parent node has a value less than or equal to the values of its children. As a result, the smallest element in the entire collection always sits at the root node.

Imagine a golf tournament leaderboard where the lowest score wins. The player with the lowest strokes sits at the very top of the pyramid, and every bracket level below holds players with progressively higher stroke counts. Whenever you want to know who is in first place with the lowest number, you just look at the top.


Understanding the Basic Terminology

Min-Heap Property: The rule that parent <= child for every node in the heap.

Extract-Min: Removing and returning the minimum element from the root in O(log n) time.

Decrease-Key: Lowering the value of an existing node, which may cause it to bubble upward toward the root.


How Does a Min-Heap Work Conceptually?

When a new value is inserted at the bottom of a min-heap, it compares itself against its parent. If the new value is *smaller* than its parent, they swap places, and the new value continues bubbling up until it meets a parent that is smaller than or equal to it.

When the minimum value at the root is extracted, the last element in the array moves to the root and sifts downward, always swapping with whichever of its two children is *smaller* until order is restored.


Where Are Min-Heaps Used in the Real World?

Shortest-Path Navigation (Dijkstra's Algorithm): Always expand the closest unvisited intersection with the smallest cumulative travel time.

Task Timers and Cron Schedulers: Keep the event with the earliest upcoming timestamp at the root so the system knows exactly how long to sleep.

Merging K Sorted Streams: Combine dozens of sorted log files in chronological order by always pulling the earliest timestamp from a min-heap.


Summary

A min-heap keeps the smallest value permanently stationed at the root, making it the go-to structure for finding shortest paths, earliest deadlines, and minimum costs.
